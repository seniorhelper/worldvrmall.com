AR and VR shopping in 2026: what shoppers actually do

The 2026 numbers on AR and VR shopping: how many people use it, on which devices, and what that means for a small store deciding where to show up.

  • Updated November 22, 2026
  • 3 min read

Short answer

Six in ten consumers have tried AR or VR, but only about a third use it regularly, and most do it on a phone, not a headset (Clutch survey of 400 consumers, March 2026). Headset shipments fell in 2025 and again in 2026 while smart glasses grew. For a small store, phone-first 3D beats headset-only.

Immersive shopping gets two kinds of coverage: hype about the "future of retail" and eulogies for the metaverse. Neither helps a shop owner decide what to do this year. Here are the most recent numbers we could verify, what each one actually measures, and what they mean in practice. Every source below was opened and re-checked on November 22, 2026.

How many people use AR or VR at all?

In a survey of 400 consumers, Clutch found that 60% had used AR or VR technology, but only 35% used it regularly. Gaming was the top reason for both. Among everyday uses of AR, social media filters and content creation came first (32%), with online shopping close behind (30%) (Clutch, "The AR/VR Advantage", updated March 17, 2026).

The number to notice is the phone one. Most people's "immersive" experience is a camera filter or a 3D view on a phone screen. Any plan that starts with "first, buy a headset" loses most shoppers at step one.

Are VR headsets catching on?

Not right now. IDC reported that shipments of Meta's Quest headsets fell 42.3% in 2025, even though the wider XR market grew 44.4% thanks to smart glasses (IDC, March 23, 2026). A second tracker, Counterpoint Research, put global VR headset shipments down 18% year over year in the second quarter of 2026, as reported by PC Gamer on September 10, 2026. Different trackers count different devices, so the exact percentages differ, but the direction is the same: fewer headsets, more glasses.

That doesn't make VR useless for shopping. People who own a headset are often keen to use it. It means VR should be a bonus mode on top of something that works on a phone and a laptop, which is how our VR mall is built.

Do shoppers want virtual try-ons?

Less than retailers hoped. In a Bizrate Insights survey for EMARKETER of 1,004 US adults aged 18 to 65 (February 2 to March 1, 2026), the most common answer was outright rejection: 38.3% of US digital shoppers said they had never used virtual or augmented reality to shop and weren't interested in trying. EMARKETER put regular use of these tools at 4.5% (EMARKETER, August 3, 2026).

The two surveys ask different questions (any AR/VR use, including games, versus shopping with it), which is why the numbers look so far apart. Read together, they say the same thing: people use immersive features when they're quick and on a phone, not when they're a chore.

What should a small store do with these numbers?

  1. Start where shoppers are. A 3D storefront has to load fast in a phone browser with no app. If it needs a download, most visitors won't see it.
  2. Make 3D the hook, not the checkout. Let people browse a walk-in store, then send the "buy" tap to the checkout they already trust: yours.
  3. Offer VR as an extra. Headset owners get the full walk-through; everyone else gets the same store on a screen.
  4. Keep a flat version. Some visitors just want a list. Our Mall feed shows every store and offer without the 3D engine.

What these numbers don't tell you

Survey samples here are modest (400 people for Clutch, 1,004 for EMARKETER) and the definitions differ from one source to the next: "used AR/VR" includes games and filters, not only shopping. Treat them as direction, not precision. We'll update this page when new full-year figures come out.
